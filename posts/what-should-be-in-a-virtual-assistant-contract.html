What Should Be in a Virtual Assistant Contract: A Practical Checklist

If you're a freelance virtual assistant, your contract isn't just paperwork — it's the document that protects your income, your time, and your client relationships. Yet many VAs either skip contracts entirely or copy a generic template that doesn't cover the specific realities of VA work: scope creep, timezone issues, access to client accounts, and the awkward moment when a client "forgets" to pay.

This guide breaks down exactly what should be in a virtual assistant contract, section by section, with examples you can adapt. By the end, you'll know what to include, what to watch out for, and how to stop losing billable hours to admin chaos.

---

Why a VA Contract Matters More Than You Think

A contract does three jobs:

1. Sets expectations — so neither side is guessing about deliverables, deadlines, or availability.

2. Protects you legally — especially around confidentiality, data access, and liability.

3. Prevents scope creep — the silent killer of VA profitability.

Without one, you're relying on memory and goodwill. That works until it doesn't.

---

The Core Sections Every VA Contract Needs

1. Parties and Effective Date

Start simple: full legal names, business names, addresses, and the date the agreement begins. If you operate as an LLC or sole proprietor, state that clearly.

Example:

> This Agreement is entered into on [Date] between [Client Name] ("Client") and [Your Business Name] ("Virtual Assistant").

2. Scope of Services

This is the most important section, and the one most VAs get wrong. Don't write "general administrative support." Be specific.

Include:

    • A bulleted list of services (e.g., inbox management, calendar scheduling, social media posting, CRM updates)
    • What is explicitly excluded (e.g., "This agreement does not include graphic design, paid ad management, or bookkeeping")
    • How new tasks are handled (e.g., "Additional services require a written addendum or a new Statement of Work")

Pro tip: Add a line like: "Tasks not listed above are out of scope and may be quoted separately." This single sentence prevents most scope creep.

3. Deliverables and Turnaround Times

Vague deadlines create friction. Specify:

    • What you deliver (e.g., "weekly content calendar," "daily inbox triage")
    • When you deliver it (e.g., "within 24 business hours of request")
    • Your working hours and timezone

Example:

> Standard turnaround for task requests is 24 business hours. Rush requests are subject to availability and may incur a 25% surcharge.

4. Compensation and Payment Terms

Be blunt here. Include:

    • Hourly rate, retainer amount, or project fee
    • Billing frequency (weekly, bi-weekly, monthly)
    • Payment method (bank transfer, PayPal, Wise, etc.)
    • Payment due date (e.g., "Net 7" — payment due within 7 days)
    • Late fees (e.g., "1.5% monthly interest on overdue balances")
    • Whether unused retainer hours roll over

Example clause:

> Client agrees to pay $X per hour, invoiced every two weeks. Payment is due within 7 days of invoice date. Late payments accrue a 5% late fee after 7 days.

5. Revisions and Rework Policy

How many rounds of revisions are included? What counts as a revision vs. a new task?

Example:

> Each deliverable includes up to two rounds of revisions. Additional revisions are billed at the standard hourly rate.

6. Confidentiality and Data Protection

You'll likely have access to email, banking dashboards, client databases, and more. Protect both sides:

    • Client information stays confidential
    • You won't share login credentials
    • You'll use password managers or secure tools
    • What happens to data when the contract ends

Example:

> The Virtual Assistant agrees to keep all client information confidential and to use secure password management tools. Upon termination, all credentials will be revoked and any stored data deleted within 7 days.

7. Independent Contractor Status

State clearly that you are not an employee. This matters for taxes and legal liability.

Example:

> The Virtual Assistant is an independent contractor and is responsible for their own taxes, insurance, and equipment.

8. Intellectual Property

Who owns the work you create? Usually the client owns final deliverables once payment is received — but you may want to retain rights to your own templates, systems, and processes.

Example:

> Upon full payment, the Client owns all final deliverables. The Virtual Assistant retains ownership of their internal templates, workflows, and methodologies.

9. Termination Clause

Both sides need an exit ramp. Include:

    • Notice period (e.g., 14 or 30 days)
    • What happens to outstanding invoices
    • Whether work in progress is billed

Example:

> Either party may terminate this agreement with 14 days' written notice. Work completed up to the termination date remains billable.

10. Limitation of Liability

Cap your liability. You don't want to be on the hook for a client's lost revenue because of a scheduling error.

Example:

> The Virtual Assistant's total liability under this agreement shall not exceed the total fees paid in the previous 30 days.

11. Dispute Resolution

Specify how disputes are handled — mediation, arbitration, or small claims court — and which jurisdiction applies.

---

Optional but Smart Additions

Depending on your niche, consider adding:

    • Non-solicitation clause — prevents clients from poaching your subcontractors
    • Non-compete clause — limits working with direct competitors (use sparingly; it can scare clients off)
    • NDA addendum — for clients handling sensitive data
    • Subcontractor clause — if you use a team, disclose it and set expectations

---

Common Mistakes VAs Make in Contracts

    • Copying a generic freelance template that doesn't address VA-specific issues like tool access or timezone coverage
    • Skipping the exclusions list — then getting stuck doing unpaid work
    • No late fee — then chasing invoices for weeks
    • No termination clause — then being trapped with a bad client
    • Verbal agreements — "We'll figure it out as we go" is not a contract

---

A Quick Pre-Send Checklist

Before you send your contract to a client, confirm it includes:

    • [ ] Full legal names and effective date
    • [ ] Detailed scope of services + exclusions
    • [ ] Deliverables and turnaround times
    • [ ] Rates, payment terms, and late fees
    • [ ] Revision policy
    • [ ] Confidentiality and data handling
    • [ ] Independent contractor status
    • [ ] IP ownership
    • [ ] Termination terms
    • [ ] Liability cap
    • [ ] Dispute resolution
    • [ ] Signature block for both parties
