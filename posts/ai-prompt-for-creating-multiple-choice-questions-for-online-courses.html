AI Prompt for Creating Multiple Choice Questions for Online Courses: A Practical Guide

Multiple choice questions (MCQs) are the workhorse of online course assessments. They're fast to grade, easy to scale, and — when written well — genuinely measure whether learners understood your material. The problem? Writing dozens (or hundreds) of them by hand is slow, repetitive, and surprisingly hard to do well.

That's where a well-crafted AI prompt for creating multiple choice questions for online courses changes the game. In this guide, you'll get concrete prompt structures, real examples, a quality checklist, and the mistakes that trip most course creators up.

---

Why Most AI-Generated MCQs Fall Flat

If you've tried asking ChatGPT or Claude to "write 10 quiz questions about my lesson," you've probably seen the results: vague stems, obvious wrong answers, and questions that test trivia instead of understanding.

The issue isn't the AI — it's the prompt. Generic prompts produce generic output. To get assessment-quality questions, your prompt needs to specify:

    • The learning objective each question should test
    • The cognitive level (recall vs. application vs. analysis)
    • The distractor strategy (what makes wrong answers plausible)
    • Formatting and structure (number of options, answer key, explanations)
    • Source material (paste your lesson content directly)

Let's build a prompt that does all of this.

---

The Core Prompt Structure (Copy This)

Here's a reusable template you can adapt for any course:

```

You are an instructional designer creating assessment items for an online course.

CONTEXT:

    • Course: [Course name]
    • Module/Lesson: [Lesson title]
    • Target audience: [e.g., beginner marketing managers]
    • Learning objective: [e.g., "Learners can identify the three components of a value proposition"]

SOURCE MATERIAL:

[Paste the lesson text, transcript, or key points here]

TASK:

Write [number] multiple choice questions that assess the objective above.

REQUIREMENTS:

1. Each question has exactly 4 answer options (A–D), with one correct answer.

2. At least 2 questions should test application (scenario-based), not just recall.

3. Distractors must be plausible — based on common misconceptions, not obviously wrong.

4. Avoid "all of the above" and "none of the above."

5. Vary the position of the correct answer across questions.

6. For each question, provide:

    • The question stem
    • Four options
    • Correct answer
    • A one-sentence explanation of why it's correct
    • A one-sentence note on why the most tempting distractor is wrong

OUTPUT FORMAT:

Numbered list. Keep stems under 25 words where possible.

```

This structure works because it forces the AI to think like an assessment designer, not a content generator.

---

Real Example: Before and After

Weak prompt: "Write 5 multiple choice questions about email marketing."

Result: Questions like "What is email marketing?" — useless for assessment.

Strong prompt (using the template above):

```

Learning objective: Learners can choose the appropriate email type for a given

customer journey stage.

Source material: [Lesson text on welcome sequences, abandoned cart emails,

re-engagement campaigns, and post-purchase follow-ups]

```

Result:

> Q1. A customer adds a product to their cart but leaves your site without purchasing. Which email type is most appropriate to send within 24 hours?

> A) Welcome sequence

> B) Abandoned cart email ✓

> C) Re-engagement campaign

> D) Post-purchase follow-up

>

> Explanation: Abandoned cart emails target users who showed purchase intent but didn't complete checkout. The tempting distractor (C) applies to inactive subscribers, not active shoppers.

That's a question that actually tests judgment.

5 Prompt Variations for Different Question Types

Not every MCQ should look the same. Use these variations to keep assessments engaging:

1. Scenario-Based Application

> "Write a short workplace scenario (2–3 sentences) and ask which action the learner should take, based on [concept]."

2. Misconception-Catcher

> "Identify the 3 most common misconceptions about [topic]. Write one question per misconception where the misconception is the tempting distractor."

3. Definition Discrimination

> "Write questions that require distinguishing between [term A] and [term B], which learners often confuse."

4. Data/Chart Interpretation

> "Provide a simple table of [metrics]. Write questions asking learners to interpret which conclusion is supported."

5. Best-Answer (Not Just Correct-Answer)

> "Write questions where multiple options are technically true, but only one is the best answer given [constraint]."

---

Quality Control Checklist

Before you accept any AI-generated question, run it through this filter:

    • [ ] Does it test the stated objective? If not, cut it.
    • [ ] Is the stem clear on first read? No trick wording.
    • [ ] Is there exactly one defensibly correct answer? Ambiguity kills trust.
    • [ ] Are distractors plausible but clearly wrong to someone who learned the material?
    • [ ] Is the correct answer position varied? (Not always B.)
    • [ ] Does it avoid "gotcha" trivia? Test understanding, not memorization of page numbers.
    • [ ] Would you be comfortable defending this question to a learner who challenged it?

If a question fails two or more checks, regenerate it with a more specific prompt.

---

Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. Letting AI write questions without your source material. Always paste the actual lesson content. Otherwise the AI invents facts.

2. Skipping the answer explanation. Explanations turn quizzes into learning moments.

3. Accepting the first batch. Generate 15 questions to get 10 good ones.

4. Forgetting accessibility. Avoid "which of the following is NOT" phrasing when possible — it increases cognitive load.

5. No difficulty mix. A good quiz has ~60% easy/medium and ~40% challenging items.

Putting It All Together

Here's your workflow:

1. Pick one lesson and write down its single most important learning objective.

2. Paste the lesson content into your AI tool of choice.

3. Use the core prompt template above, adjusting the cognitive level.

4. Generate 1.5x the questions you need, then filter with the checklist.

5. Add explanations to every question — this is non-negotiable.

6. Test the quiz yourself before publishing. If you hesitate on an answer, rewrite it.
