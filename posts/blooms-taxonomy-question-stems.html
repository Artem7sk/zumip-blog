Bloom's Taxonomy Question Stems: A Practical Guide for K–12 Teachers

If you've ever stared at a blank lesson plan wondering how to push students past simple recall, Bloom's taxonomy question stems are one of the fastest tools you can reach for. They turn vague verbs like "understand" or "analyze" into concrete prompts you can actually say, print, or paste into a quiz.

This guide walks through what Bloom's taxonomy question stems are, how to use them at each cognitive level, and how to pair them with differentiation so one objective can serve your struggling readers, your on-level students, and your high flyers at the same time.

---

What Are Bloom's Taxonomy Question Stems?

Bloom's taxonomy is a framework for classifying the cognitive demand of learning tasks. In its revised form (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001), it has six levels:

1. Remember – recall facts and basic concepts

2. Understand – explain ideas or concepts

3. Apply – use information in new situations

4. Analyze – draw connections among ideas

5. Evaluate – justify a stand or decision

6. Create – produce new or original work

A question stem is the opening phrase of a question that sets its cognitive demand. For example:

    • Remember: "List three…"
    • Analyze: "What is the relationship between…?"
    • Evaluate: "What criteria would you use to judge…?"

The stem does most of the work. Swap the content, keep the stem, and you have a question at the right level — for any subject, any grade.

---

Why Question Stems Matter More Than Verb Lists

Most teachers have seen the verb charts: identify, describe, compare, evaluate. The problem is that verbs are ambiguous. "Describe" can be a one-word answer in 1st grade or a full paragraph in 10th.

Question stems fix that. They:

    • Force specificity. "What is the relationship between ___ and ___?" can't be answered with a single word.
    • Speed up planning. You stop reinventing question wording every unit.
    • Make differentiation mechanical. Same stem, different text complexity or scaffold.
    • Support assessment alignment. If your objective says "analyze," your exit ticket should use an analyze-level stem — not a recall one.

Bloom's Taxonomy Question Stems by Level (With Examples)

Below are stems you can use immediately. All examples use a generic "___" so you can drop in your own content.

1. Remember — Retrieval

Stems:

    • List the ___ of ___.
    • Name three ___.
    • Who/What/Where/When was ___?
    • Define ___ in your own words.

Example (Grade 4 science): List the three states of matter and give one example of each.

Watch out: This level is necessary but shouldn't dominate. If every exit ticket is a remember-level question, you're assessing memory, not learning.

2. Understand — Explanation

Stems:

    • Explain why ___ happened.
    • Summarize ___ in two sentences.
    • What is the main idea of ___?
    • How would you describe ___ to someone younger?

Example (Grade 7 ELA): Summarize the narrator's main conflict in two sentences.

3. Apply — Transfer

Stems:

    • Use ___ to solve ___.
    • What would happen if you applied ___ to ___?
    • Show how ___ can be used to ___.
    • Solve ___ using the method we practiced.

Example (Grade 9 math): Use the Pythagorean theorem to find the missing side of this triangle.

4. Analyze — Structure and Relationships

Stems:

    • What is the relationship between ___ and ___?
    • Compare and contrast ___ and ___.
    • What evidence supports ___?
    • What caused ___? What was the effect?
    • How is ___ organized? Why?

Example (Grade 6 social studies): Compare and contrast the economies of the Northern and Southern colonies. What caused the differences?

5. Evaluate — Judgment With Criteria

Stems:

    • What criteria would you use to judge ___?
    • Which is more effective, ___ or ___? Defend your choice.
    • What are the strengths and weaknesses of ___?
    • Do you agree with ___? Why or why not?

Example (Grade 11 biology): Which of the two proposed solutions to antibiotic resistance is more effective? Defend your choice with evidence.

6. Create — Synthesis

Stems:

    • Design a ___ that ___.
    • Propose an alternative to ___.
    • Write a ___ from the perspective of ___.
    • What would happen if ___? Construct a scenario.

Example (Grade 5 ELA): Write an alternate ending to the story from the antagonist's perspective.

How to Use Question Stems in Real Lessons

Knowing the stems is step one. Here's how to actually build them into your week.

Step 1: Start With the Objective

Write your objective using a Bloom's verb. If your standard says "students will analyze," your questions should sit at analyze or above — not at remember.

Step 2: Build a Question Ladder

For each lesson, plan 3–5 questions that climb the taxonomy:

1. One remember or understand question (entry point)

2. One apply question (practice)

3. One analyze or evaluate question (the real target)

4. Optional create extension for early finishers

This ladder doubles as your differentiation: everyone answers 1–2, most reach 3, advanced students tackle 4.

Step 3: Match Stems to Task Type

| Task | Recommended Bloom's levels |

|---|---|

| Warm-up / Do Now | Remember, Understand |

| Guided practice | Apply |

| Exit ticket | Analyze, Evaluate |

| Quiz (mixed) | 40% Remember/Understand, 40% Apply/Analyze, 20% Evaluate/Create |

| Project / performance task | Create |

Step 4: Differentiate Without Rewriting

Here's the practical trick: keep the stem the same, change the support.

    • Struggling learners: same stem + sentence starters + word bank + shorter text
    • On-level: same stem + grade-level text
    • Advanced: same stem + more complex text + "justify with two pieces of evidence"

This is exactly the logic behind a differentiation matrix — one objective, three entry points.

A Quick Checklist Before You Print

Run every quiz or exit ticket through this list:

    • [ ] Does at least one question sit above the remember level?
    • [ ] Does each question stem match the verb in my objective?
    • [ ] Have I included a sentence starter or scaffold for students who need it?
    • [ ] Is there an extension question for students who finish early?
    • [ ] Can a student answer the top-level question in one word? (If yes, rewrite it.)
    • [ ] Do the stems vary, or did I use "What is…" five times?

If you answer "no" to any of these, revise before copying.

Common Mistakes With Bloom's Taxonomy Question Stems

1. Treating the levels as a strict ladder.

You don't have to start at remember every lesson. Sometimes you open with an evaluate question to hook students.

2. Confusing "apply" with "practice."

Applying means using knowledge in a new context. Ten identical practice problems is drill, not application.

3. Using "evaluate" stems without criteria.

"What do you think?" is not evaluation. "What criteria would you use to judge…?" is.

4. Ignoring the reading level.

A perfectly crafted analyze-level question is useless if the text is three grade levels above the student. Differentiation has to happen on both axes — cognitive demand and text complexity.

5. Rebuilding everything from scratch.

This is the biggest time sink. Most teachers don't need new question stems — they need a reusable bank organized by level and a template to plug them into.

Where Question Stems Fit Into Differentiation

Differentiation has two levers:

1. Cognitive demand — controlled by the Bloom's level of your question stem

2. Access — controlled by text complexity, scaffolds, and supports

Most teachers adjust one lever and ignore the other. Strong differentiation moves both.

For example, a 7th-grade objective like "Analyze how an author develops a theme" can be served by:

    • Tier 1 (scaffolded): Analyze-level stem + shortened passage + sentence frame: "The author develops the theme of ___ by ___."
    • Tier 2 (on-level): Analyze-level stem + full passage + "cite two details."
    • Tier 3 (extension): Evaluate-level stem + full passage + "compare how two authors develop the same theme."

Same objective. Same class period. Three entry points.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are Bloom's taxonomy question stems only for tests?

No. Use them for discussion prompts, exit tickets, warm-ups, Socratic seminars, and project checkpoints.

How many levels should a single quiz cover?

Aim for at least three. A quiz that's all remember-level questions tells you who memorized, not who learned.

Do I need to use all six levels in one lesson?

No. One or two well-chosen levels per lesson is plenty. Coverage across a unit matters more than coverage within a single period.

What about ELLs and students with IEPs?

Keep the cognitive demand high and lower the language barrier — sentence frames, visuals, glossaries, chunked text. Don't drop to remember-level questions just because the text is hard.

Can I use these stems for any subject?

Yes. The stems are content-agnostic. Only the "___" changes.
