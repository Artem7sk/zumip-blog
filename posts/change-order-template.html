Change Order Template: How Contractors Stop Losing Money on Scope Changes

Ask any contractor what kills their margins, and you'll hear the same answer: change orders. Not the big, obvious ones — the small verbal "while you're here" requests that never get documented, never get priced properly, and never get paid in full. A solid change order template is the difference between a profitable project and one you finish at a loss. This guide breaks down exactly what your template needs, how to use it, and how to stop the bleeding.

What Is a Change Order Template (and Why Most Contractors Get It Wrong)

A change order template is a standardized document that captures any modification to the original scope, schedule, or price of a project. It protects both parties: the client gets clarity on what's changing and what it costs, and you get written authorization before you lift a finger.

Most contractors either skip this document entirely or use a stripped-down version that misses critical fields. The result? Disputes, delayed payments, and work performed for free.

Here's the uncomfortable math: if you complete just three uncharged change orders per month at $400 each, that's $14,400 in lost revenue per year. For a small crew, that's often the entire profit margin on several jobs.

The 9 Fields Every Change Order Template Must Include

A change order template is only as good as the information it forces you to capture. Here's what belongs in yours:

1. Change order number and date — Sequential numbering keeps your records clean and makes it easy to reference in invoices.

2. Project name and address — Never assume context. Be specific.

3. Original contract reference — Tie the change back to the signed agreement.

4. Description of the change — What exactly is being added, removed, or modified. Be detailed enough that a third party could understand it.

5. Reason for the change — Client request, unforeseen condition, design revision, code requirement. This matters for disputes.

6. Cost breakdown — Labor, materials, equipment, subcontractors, and markup. Itemize everything.

7. Schedule impact — Does this add days to the timeline? State it explicitly.

8. New contract total — Show the revised grand total, not just the delta.

9. Signature lines for both parties — No signature, no enforceable change order.

If your current template is missing any of these, you're leaving yourself exposed.

Step-by-Step: How to Process a Change Order Without Losing Money

Step 1: Document the Request Immediately

The moment a client asks for something outside the original scope, write it down. Date it. Note who requested it and how (email, text, in person). Verbal requests are the number one source of unpaid work.

Step 2: Assess the Real Cost — Including Material Fluctuations

This is where most contractors underbid. You estimate based on today's material prices, but the work happens weeks later. Lumber, copper, concrete, and steel prices can swing significantly in that window.

Your change order template should include a line for material cost escalation or a stated validity period (e.g., "pricing valid for 14 days"). If you're not accounting for this, you're absorbing the difference.

Step 3: Price the Change With Proper Markup

Don't just pass through cost. Apply your standard overhead and profit margin. A change order isn't a favor — it's additional work that carries the same risk and overhead as the original contract.

A simple formula:

    • Direct costs (labor + materials + equipment)
    • + Overhead percentage (typically 10–20%)
    • + Profit margin (typically 10–20%)
    • = Change order price

Step 4: Get Written Approval Before Starting

Send the change order, get the signature, then schedule the work. No exceptions. Contractors who start work "to keep the client happy" and paper it later routinely get stuck eating the cost.

Step 5: Track Every Change Order in One Place

Keep a running log. At project closeout, you should be able to show the original contract, every approved change order, and the final total. This protects you during final payment negotiations.

Common Mistakes That Cost Contractors Thousands

    • Using a verbal agreement — "He said it was fine" doesn't hold up.
    • Bundling changes into the final invoice — Clients forget, dispute, or refuse.
    • Forgetting schedule impact — A two-day delay caused by a change can trigger liquidated damages on some contracts.
    • No expiration date on pricing — Material costs move. Your quotes should too.
    • Not numbering change orders — Makes tracking and auditing a nightmare.

Quick Checklist Before You Send Any Change Order

    • [ ] Change order number assigned
    • [ ] Original contract referenced
    • [ ] Scope change described in detail
    • [ ] Labor, materials, and equipment itemized
    • [ ] Markup applied
    • [ ] Schedule impact stated
    • [ ] New contract total calculated
    • [ ] Pricing validity period included
    • [ ] Signature lines for both parties
    • [ ] Copy saved to project file
