How to Vet Dropshipping Suppliers Before Placing a Test Order

A test order is the moment of truth. You've picked a product, built the store, and now you're about to send real money to a supplier you've never met. Get it wrong and you've burned cash, lost a customer, and wasted a week. Get it right and you have a repeatable supplier you can scale with.

The problem is that most beginners vet suppliers after the test order fails — when refunds, chargebacks, and angry emails are already in motion. This guide walks through a practical vetting process you can run before you spend a dollar, so your test order is a confirmation step, not a gamble.

Why the Test Order Isn't the Vetting Step

A test order tells you three things: whether the product arrives, how long it takes, and what the packaging looks like. It does not tell you:

    • Whether the supplier can handle 50 orders a week instead of one
    • Whether they'll honor a defect claim without ghosting you
    • Whether their listed "US warehouse" is actually a drop-ship relay with a 12-day domestic delay
    • Whether their price leaves you any margin after ad spend

Treat the test order as the final check, not the first one. Everything below happens before it.

Step 1: Screen the Supplier's Storefront and Profile

Start with the platforms most dropshippers use: AliExpress, CJdropshipping, Spocket, Zendrop, Alibaba, or a private agent. Each has signals you can read in 10 minutes.

On AliExpress and similar marketplaces, check:

    • Store age and transaction volume. A store open 4+ years with consistent order history is a different risk profile than one opened 6 months ago.
    • Rating breakdown, not just the headline score. A 96% positive rating with recent 1-star reviews about shipping is worse than a 94% rating with steady 5-star reviews.
    • Response time in the chat. Message them a specific question ("Can you ship this SKU from a US warehouse to California in under 7 days?"). Time how long the reply takes and whether it actually answers the question.
    • Product photo consistency. If the same photos appear across 20 unrelated stores, you're looking at a reseller of a reseller.

On Alibaba or with private agents, check:

    • Whether they're a trading company or a manufacturer. Ask directly. Trading companies aren't automatically bad, but they add a markup layer and a communication layer.
    • Whether they can provide a business license and a real factory address.
    • Whether they'll quote MOQs and lead times in writing.

Step 2: Ask the Questions That Reveal Real Operations

Vague suppliers give vague answers. Send a short, specific list and see how they respond. Good suppliers answer in detail; bad ones copy-paste a generic reply.

Send something like this:

1. What is your average processing time from order to shipment?

2. Which carriers do you use, and do you have tracking that updates within 48 hours?

3. Do you ship from a US/EU warehouse for this SKU, or only from China?

4. What's your defect rate on this product, and how do you handle replacements?

5. Can you do custom packaging or insert cards, and at what cost?

6. What's your price at 10, 50, and 200 units per month?

The answers matter less than the pattern. A supplier who answers 5 of 6 questions clearly is worth testing. One who answers "yes friend, all good" to everything is not.

Step 3: Score the Supplier on a Realistic Margin Model

Before ordering, run the numbers. A supplier can look cheap and still kill your store.

Use this quick check:

    • Landed cost = product price + shipping + any packaging fees
    • Target sell price = what comparable stores charge (check 3–5 competitors)
    • Gross margin = (sell price − landed cost) ÷ sell price

If your gross margin is under ~3x landed cost, ad costs will eat you alive on cold traffic. Aim for at least 3x, ideally 4x, before you test.

Also factor in:

    • Payment processing fees (~2.9% + $0.30 typical)
    • Refund/return rate (assume 3–8% for physical goods)
    • Chargeback risk if shipping is slow

A supplier with a 4.5x margin and reliable shipping beats a 6x margin supplier who ships in 20 days every time.

Step 4: Cross-Check the Supplier Across Channels

Don't rely on one platform's rating. Search for the supplier name plus "review," "scam," or "experience" on Reddit (r/dropship, r/ecommerce), Trustpilot, and YouTube. Look for:

    • Repeated complaints about the same issue (slow shipping, wrong items, no refunds)
    • Whether the supplier responds publicly to complaints
    • Whether the complaints are recent or from 3 years ago

A supplier with a few old complaints and active responses is usually fine. A supplier with 15 recent complaints about the same problem is a hard pass.

Step 5: Place a Small Test Order — Correctly

Now you're ready. But test it like a customer would, not like a supplier's friend.

    • Order 2–3 units, not one. One unit doesn't reveal consistency.
    • Ship to your own address, not a friend's.
    • Use a real payment method so you experience the checkout flow.
    • Don't tell them it's a test. You want to see the default experience.
    • Time every stage: order confirmation, tracking upload, first scan, delivery.

While waiting, do a second test order to a different address if you're serious. This checks whether the first good result was luck.

Step 6: Evaluate the Test Order Against a Checklist

When the package arrives, score it:

    • [ ] Product matches the listing photos and description
    • [ ] Packaging is intact and presentable
    • [ ] No branding from the supplier or marketplace on the label
    • [ ] Delivery time matches their quoted window
    • [ ] Tracking updated at least 3 times
    • [ ] Customer support replied to a post-order question within 24 hours
    • [ ] You'd be comfortable if a real customer received this

If you check 6 of 7, you have a viable supplier. If you check fewer, either renegotiate expectations or move on.

Where Most People Get This Wrong

The most common mistake isn't picking a bad supplier — it's not having a system. People vet one supplier, get lucky, and assume the process is obvious. Then the second supplier fails and they don't know why.

The second mistake is skipping the margin math because the product "looks cool." Cool products with thin margins are the #1 reason new stores die.

The third is treating the test order as the whole vetting process. It's the last 10%.
