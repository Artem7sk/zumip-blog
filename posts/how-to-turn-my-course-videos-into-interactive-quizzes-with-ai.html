How to Turn My Course Videos Into Interactive Quizzes With AI

Your course videos are already packed with teaching moments. The problem is that most learners watch passively — they nod along, feel like they understand, and then forget 80% of it a week later. Interactive quizzes fix that. They force retrieval, surface gaps, and turn a one-way lecture into a two-way conversation.

The good news: you don't need to write hundreds of questions by hand. With AI, you can turn your existing course videos into interactive quizzes in a fraction of the time. Here's a practical, step-by-step workflow you can run this week.

---

Why Bother Converting Videos Into Quizzes?

Before we get into the "how," let's be clear on the "why." Quizzes aren't just filler.

    • Retrieval practice boosts retention. Learners who are tested on material remember it far better than those who simply re-watch it.
    • They expose confusion early. A wrong answer on question 3 tells you the learner missed a key concept in minute 4.
    • They increase completion rates. Interactive elements break up passive viewing and keep attention on the screen.
    • They give you data. Which concepts trip people up? Which lessons are too easy? Quizzes tell you what to fix.

If you're selling a course, quizzes also raise perceived value. A course with checkpoints feels like a system, not a video dump.

---

Step 1: Prepare Your Video for AI (Transcription First)

AI can't quiz what it can't read. So the first move is always a transcript.

What to do:

1. Upload your video to a transcription tool (Descript, Otter, Whisper, or the built-in captions on YouTube/Vimeo).

2. Export as plain text with timestamps if possible.

3. Clean up obvious errors — names, jargon, acronyms the tool got wrong.

4. Split the transcript by lesson or by logical section (e.g., "Intro," "Core concept," "Example," "Recap").

Pro tip: Keep the timestamps. You'll want them later to link quiz questions back to the exact moment in the video.

---

Step 2: Identify "Quiz-Worthy" Moments

Not every sentence deserves a question. You want moments that test understanding, not trivia.

Look for these patterns in your transcript:

    • Definitions — "X is defined as…"
    • Cause and effect — "When you do A, B happens because…"
    • Lists and frameworks — "There are three types of…"
    • Common mistakes — "Most people get this wrong by…"
    • Applied examples — "So if a client comes to you with X, you'd…"

Mark 5–10 of these per 10 minutes of video. That's your raw material.

---

Step 3: Write AI Prompts That Actually Work

This is where most people fail. They type "make me a quiz about this video" and get generic garbage. The fix is specificity.

A strong prompt template:

> "You are an instructional designer. Below is a transcript from a lesson on [topic] for [audience level]. Generate 5 multiple-choice questions that test comprehension of the core concepts, not memorization of trivia. Each question should have 4 options, one correct answer, and a short explanation for why the correct answer is right and why the most common wrong answer is tempting. Avoid questions whose answers can be guessed from grammar alone."

Then paste your transcript section.

Variations to try:

    • Scenario-based questions: "Write 3 questions that put the learner in a realistic situation and ask what they'd do next."
    • Misconception checks: "Identify 3 common misconceptions about this topic and write a question that exposes each one."
    • Short-answer prompts: "Write 5 open-ended questions that require a 2–3 sentence response."
    • Discussion prompts: "Write 3 prompts a cohort could debate in a forum."

You'll get dramatically better output if you specify question type, audience, difficulty, and format every single time. ---

Step 4: Review and Refine (Don't Skip This)

AI drafts are a starting point, not a finished product. Run every question through this checklist:

    • [ ] Is the correct answer actually correct based on my video, not general knowledge?
    • [ ] Are the wrong answers plausible but clearly wrong?
    • [ ] Is there only one defensible correct answer?
    • [ ] Does the question test understanding, not memory of exact wording?
    • [ ] Is the explanation genuinely helpful, or just "because that's the answer"?

Kill any question that fails two or more of these. Ten good questions beat thirty mediocre ones.

---

Step 5: Choose the Right Interactive Format

"Interactive" doesn't just mean multiple choice. Match format to learning goal:

| Goal | Best format |

|---|---|

| Recall of facts | Multiple choice, true/false |

| Application of concepts | Scenario questions, branching |

| Reflection | Short answer, journal prompts |

| Discussion | Open-ended prompts, peer review |

| Confidence check | Confidence-weighted quizzes |

If your platform supports it, embed questions inside the video at the relevant timestamp. Learners answer, then keep watching. This is the single highest-impact upgrade you can make.

---

Step 6: Add Feedback Loops

A quiz without feedback is just a test. A quiz with feedback is a lesson.

For every question, include:

    • Why the correct answer is correct (1–2 sentences).
    • Why the most common wrong answer is tempting (this is where real learning happens).
    • A pointer back to the video — "Rewatch 4:12–5:30 for the full explanation."

AI can generate all three in one pass if you ask for them explicitly in your prompt.

---

Step 7: Build a Repeatable Workflow

Once you've done this for one lesson, template it. Your workflow should look like:

1. Transcribe video → clean transcript

2. Highlight quiz-worthy moments

3. Run AI prompt → draft questions

4. Review against checklist

5. Add feedback + timestamps

6. Upload to your LMS or quiz tool

7. Track results → revise weak questions

Do this consistently and you can convert a 20-lesson course into an interactive quiz-driven experience in a weekend instead of a month.
