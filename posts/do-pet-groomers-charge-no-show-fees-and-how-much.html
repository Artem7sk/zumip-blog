Do Pet Groomers Charge No-Show Fees and How Much? A Practical Guide for Salon Owners

If you run a grooming salon, a mobile grooming van, or a boarding kennel, you already know the sting of a no-show. You blocked out a 90-minute slot, turned down other bookings, prepped the tub, and then… nobody came. So it's a fair question to ask: do pet groomers charge no-show fees, and how much should you charge?

This guide breaks down how no-show fees actually work in the pet grooming industry, what real-world pricing looks like, how to set a policy clients won't fight you on, and how to enforce it without damaging your reputation.

What Is a No-Show Fee (and How Is It Different From a Cancellation Fee)?

These two terms get used interchangeably, but they're not the same thing, and clients respond differently to each.

    • No-show fee: Charged when a client simply doesn't show up and doesn't contact you. This is the most disruptive scenario because you had zero chance to fill the slot.
    • Late cancellation fee: Charged when a client cancels within a short window (typically 24–48 hours). You had some notice, but likely not enough to rebook.
    • Same-day cancellation fee: A middle ground — often charged at a lower rate than a full no-show.

Being clear about this distinction in your policy matters. Clients who understand why a no-show costs you more than a 48-hour cancellation are far more likely to accept the fee.

Do Pet Groomers Actually Charge No-Show Fees?

Yes — and it's becoming standard practice, especially in:

    • Mobile grooming, where the groomer has already driven to the client's address
    • High-demand salons with waitlists and limited appointment slots
    • Boarding kennels during peak seasons (holidays, summer)

That said, not every groomer charges. Some solo groomers avoid fees to stay "friendly" and competitive, then absorb the loss. The problem is that this approach usually leads to burnout and resentment — and it doesn't actually protect your business.

The trend is clearly moving toward formal policies, because no-shows aren't just annoying — they're a direct hit to revenue.

How Much Do Groomers Typically Charge for No-Shows?

There's no single industry standard, but here's the range you'll see most often:

| Fee Type | Typical Range |

|---|---|

| No-show fee (flat) | $25–$50 |

| No-show fee (percentage) | 25%–50% of the service price |

| Late cancellation (24–48 hrs) | $15–$30 |

| Same-day cancellation | 50% of service price |

| Mobile grooming no-show | $50–$75 (covers travel) |

A few rules of thumb that work well:

1. Flat fees are easier to communicate and less likely to trigger arguments than percentages.

2. Mobile groomers should charge more, because fuel and drive time are already spent.

3. First-time offenders can get a warning — repeat offenders should not.

4. The fee should hurt a little, not a lot. If it's too high, clients leave. If it's too low, they ignore it.

If you're unsure where to start, a $35 flat no-show fee with a clear written policy is a reasonable, defensible baseline for most small salons.

How to Set a No-Show Policy That Clients Accept

A no-show fee only works if clients agreed to it before they booked. Here's a practical setup:

1. Put it in writing — everywhere

    • On your booking page
    • In your confirmation text/email
    • On your intake form (with a signature line)
    • On your website FAQ

2. Explain the "why" in one sentence

Instead of "No-shows will be charged $35," try:

> "Because each appointment reserves a dedicated time slot, no-shows and same-day cancellations are charged a $35 fee so we can keep serving all our clients fairly."

3. Require a card on file

You can't charge a fee if you have no payment method. Ask for a card at booking, and state clearly that it will only be charged in the case of a no-show or late cancellation.

4. Apply the policy consistently

The fastest way to lose client trust is to enforce the fee for one person and waive it for another. Consistency is the policy.

A Step-by-Step No-Show Response Workflow

When a client no-shows, don't react emotionally. Follow a script:

Step 1 — Wait 15 minutes, then reach out.

> "Hi [Name], we had [Pet] scheduled for 10:00 AM today and we haven't seen you yet. Everything okay? Let us know if you'd like to reschedule."

Step 2 — If no response within 24 hours, send the fee notice.

> "Hi [Name], since we didn't hear from you and the appointment time was held for [Pet], we've applied the $35 no-show fee per our policy. We'd love to get [Pet] rebooked — here's our calendar link."

Step 3 — Log it.

Track every no-show in a simple spreadsheet: client name, date, service, fee charged (Y/N), notes. Patterns will emerge fast.

Step 4 — Set a repeat-offender rule.

Many groomers use a "three strikes" approach: warning, fee, then prepayment required for future bookings.

Common Objections (and How to Handle Them)

"I forgot — can you waive it just this once?"

> "I understand — it happens. As a one-time courtesy I'll waive it this time, but going forward the fee applies to all no-shows so we can keep things fair."

"I didn't know about the fee."

> "It's listed on your booking confirmation and intake form — I'm happy to resend it so it's clear for next time."

"That's too much money."

> "The fee covers the time slot we held for [Pet]. If it helps, we can apply it as a credit toward your next grooming appointment."

That last option — converting the fee into a credit — is a smart compromise. You still discourage no-shows, but you keep the client and their future revenue.

Should You Ever Waive the Fee?

Yes, in these cases:

    • Genuine emergencies (medical, family, weather)
    • First-time clients who clearly misunderstood the policy
    • Long-time loyal clients with a clean record

But never waive it silently. Always say: "I'm waiving it this one time." That way, the policy stays intact and the client knows it's a favor, not a loophole.

The Bottom Line

So, do pet groomers charge no-show fees, and how much? Yes — most professional groomers do, and typical fees range from $25 to $75, depending on whether you're a salon or mobile service. The exact number matters less than having a clear, consistent, well-communicated policy that clients agreed to upfront.

A no-show policy isn't about punishing clients — it's about protecting your time, your income, and your ability to serve the clients who do show up. Pair it with reminder texts, card-on-file, and firm-but-friendly scripts, and no-shows stop being a recurring headache.
